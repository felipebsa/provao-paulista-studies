# Chemistry (1st year)

## Pure substances and mixtures

- A homogeneous mixture has one phase only (air, mineral water, 70% alcohol). A heterogeneous one has two or more visible phases (water and oil, water and sand).
- Water with ice cubes looks like a mixture, but it is just H₂O in two states. It is a pure substance in a system with two phases.

## Separating mixtures

| Mixture | Method |
|---|---|
| Solid that doesn't dissolve + liquid (sand and water) | filtration |
| Two liquids that don't mix (water and oil) | decanting (separatory funnel) |
| Dissolved solid + liquid, when you want the liquid back | simple distillation |
| Two liquids that mix (water and alcohol) | fractional distillation |
| Dissolved solid, when you don't need the liquid | evaporation |

When the question gives a table with melting point, boiling point and "soluble/immiscible", I look at the column about water first. Immiscible with water means separatory funnel. To know if a substance is solid at 25 °C, I check if its melting point is above 25 °C. If it is, and it doesn't dissolve, the answer is filtration.

## States of matter and heating curves

On the heating curve of a pure substance, the sloped lines are heating of one phase only (solid, liquid or gas), and the temperature goes up. The flat parts are changes of state, melting and boiling. During a flat part the temperature stays the same until the change is finished.

## Density and interface

d = mass ÷ volume.

Liquids that don't mix form layers, with the least dense on top. The interface is the line between two layers. An object with a density between the two liquids sinks in the upper one and floats on the lower one, so it stops exactly at the interface.

Example: liquids X (0.80), Y (1.00) and Z (1.25 g/cm³). A sphere of 0.92 sinks in X and floats on Y, so it stays between X and Y. A sphere of 1.15 sinks in X and Y, floats on Z and stays between Y and Z.

## Atomic models

- Dalton: solid, indivisible sphere.
- Thomson: positive sphere with electrons spread in it ("plum pudding").
- Rutherford: small, dense nucleus with electrons around it and lots of empty space (gold foil experiment).
- Bohr: electrons sit in energy levels. When an electron gets energy it jumps to a farther level, and when it comes back it releases that energy as light. That's why fireworks and flares have colors.

Isotopes have the same number of protons. Isobars have the same mass. Isotones have the same number of neutrons.

## Bonds and ions

- Metal + nonmetal: ionic bond (NaCl). The metal loses electrons and becomes a cation, the nonmetal gains them and becomes an anion.
- Nonmetal + nonmetal: covalent (H₂O, CO₂).
- Metal + metal: metallic.
- On the periodic table, the left side is metals and the right side is nonmetals (hydrogen is the exception).
- Group 1 (alkali metals): 1 electron in the outer shell, they lose 1 and become a 1+ cation.
- Group 2 (alkaline earth metals, like Mg, Ca and Ba): 2 electrons in the outer shell, they lose 2 and become a 2+ cation.
- Group 17 (halogens: F, Cl, Br, I): 7 electrons in the outer shell, they gain 1 and become a 1− anion.
- Group 18 (noble gases: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe): the outer shell is already full, with 8 electrons (2 for helium). So they barely react and show up as single atoms, without forming molecules.
- To build an ionic formula, the charges have to cancel out: Mg²⁺ with Cl⁻ becomes MgCl₂, and Ba²⁺ with Cl⁻ becomes BaCl₂. The number of one charge becomes the subscript of the other ("slide and sit").

The strongest intermolecular force between molecules with O-H, N-H or F-H is the hydrogen bond (like in cellulose and water).

## Inorganic functions and indicators

- Acid: makes the solution acidic (vinegar, HNO₃). Turns red-cabbage indicator red.
- Base: has OH (NaOH, Mg(OH)₂, NH₄OH). Turns red-cabbage indicator green.
- Salt: comes from acid + base (NaCl, KNO₃). Usually neutral in water.
- Ammonia in water makes a basic solution and raises the pH.

## Concentration

C = mass ÷ volume (g/L). What shows up the most is the dose:

1. Find the concentration: 33 g in 1 L = 33 g/L.
2. Multiply by the dose volume in liters: 60 mL = 0.06 L, so 33 × 0.06 ≈ 2 g.

Remember that 1 L = 1,000 mL and that dividing by 0.5 L is the same as multiplying by 2.

Proportion also works. Water with 30 mg/L and 750 mL drunk (3/4 of a liter): 3/4 of 30 = 22.5 mg. A 500 mL bottle with 20 g and a 25 mL dose: the dose is 20 times smaller than the bottle, so it has 20 ÷ 20 = 1 g.

Dilution: the mass of solute stays the same, it just spreads over a bigger volume.

## Stoichiometry and excess reagent

The balanced equation is the recipe, with a fixed ratio. The numbers in the problem are what's in stock, so I don't change the coefficients because of them. To find the limiting reagent, I divide the moles of each reagent by its coefficient. The smallest result is the limiting one, and it decides how much product I get. Example with 3 Si + 4 NH₃ → Si₃N₄ + 6 H₂, with 12 mol of each:

- 12 mol of NH₃ would need 9 mol of Si, so there is Si left over.
- The 12 mol of NH₃ run out first (limiting reagent) and make 12 ÷ 4 = 3 mol of Si₃N₄.
- 12 − 9 = 3 mol of Si are left.

I always figure out which one runs out first.

## Lavoisier's law

In a closed container, the mass of the reactants is equal to the mass of the products. In an open container, if a gas escapes (CO₂, O₂), the scale shows less because the gas went into the air. The law still holds, the mass that left the scale is in the atmosphere.

## Combustion

- Complete (plenty of oxygen): only CO₂ and H₂O, blue flame.
- Incomplete (little oxygen): also releases CO and soot (C), yellow flame and black smoke. The dark smoke from a diesel truck is this.

## Physical and chemical change

- Physical: the substance stays the same. Change of state, butter melting, water evaporating, salt dissolving.
- Chemical: a new substance forms. Gas burning, fruit turning brown, steel wool rusting.

## Safety

- Flame on the pictogram: flammable (alcohol, acetone).
- Hand and surface being eaten away: corrosive (caustic soda).

## Environment

- Acid rain comes from sulfur and nitrogen oxides (SO₂, NOx).
- Greenhouse effect: CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide and water vapor.
- CO₂ dissolved in the ocean makes the water more acidic, so the pH goes down.

## Practice questions

### Question 1

SiO₂ (s) + 3 C (s) → SiC (s) + 2 CO (g)

When 4 mol of SiO₂ and 9 mol of C are mixed, how much SiC is formed and which reagent is in excess?

(A) 4 mol of SiC and 1 mol of C
(B) 3 mol of SiC and 1 mol of SiO₂
(C) 3 mol of SiC and 3 mol of C
(D) 4 mol of SiC and no excess
(E) 9 mol of SiC and 5 mol of SiO₂

**Answer: B.** Every 3 mol of C need 1 mol of SiO₂. With 9 mol of C, 3 mol of SiO₂ are used and 3 mol of SiC are made. There were 4 mol of SiO₂, so 1 mol is left. Carbon is the limiting reagent.

### Question 2

Barium (Ba, Z = 56) belongs to the family of ______ metals. To become stable, a barium atom ______ 2 electrons. When it reacts with chlorine (group 17), it forms the ionic compound with formula ______.

(A) alkaline earth ... gains ... BaCl₂
(B) alkali ... loses ... Ba₂Cl
(C) alkaline earth ... loses ... BaCl₂
(D) alkaline earth ... loses ... Ba₂Cl
(E) alkali ... gains ... BaCl

**Answer: C.** Barium is in group 2 and, as a metal, loses the 2 electrons from its outer shell (Ba²⁺). Chlorine gains 1 (Cl⁻). Crossing the charges, the formula is BaCl₂.

### Question 3

Four substances were added to four containers of distilled water at 25 °C (d = 1.0 g/cm³):

- Substance 1: MP = −98 °C, BP = 65 °C, miscible in water.
- Substance 2: MP = 114 °C, BP = 444 °C, insoluble in water.
- Substance 3: MP = 801 °C, BP = 1413 °C, soluble in water.
- Substance 4: MP = −20 °C, BP = 75 °C, immiscible in water.

Which mixture can be separated by simple filtration (filter paper and funnel)?

(A) 1  (B) 2  (C) 3  (D) 4  (E) 1 and 3

**Answer: B.** At 25 °C substance 2 is a solid (MP of 114 °C) and it doesn't dissolve, so the mixture is solid + liquid and filtration works. Substance 1 is a miscible liquid (fractional distillation), 3 is a dissolved solid (simple distillation or evaporation) and 4 is an immiscible liquid (decanting).

### Question 4

Three liquids that don't mix are in a graduated cylinder: X (d = 0.80 g/cm³), Y (d = 1.00 g/cm³) and Z (d = 1.25 g/cm³). Two solid spheres are put in: A (d = 0.92 g/cm³) and B (d = 1.15 g/cm³). Where does each sphere end up?

(A) A floats on X and B goes to the bottom.
(B) Both sink completely.
(C) A stays at the interface between X and Y, and B at the interface between Y and Z.
(D) A floats on Y and B floats on X.
(E) A stays at the interface between Y and Z, and B floats on X.

**Answer: C.** The liquids form layers: Z at the bottom, Y in the middle, X on top. Sphere A (0.92) sinks in X and floats on Y, so it stops between X and Y. Sphere B (1.15) sinks in X and Y and floats on Z, so it stops between Y and Z.

### Question 5

In an open beaker, 100 g of acid solution reacted with 10 g of calcium carbonate, with a lot of CO₂ fizzing, and the final mass measured was 105.6 g. The experiment was repeated in a closed flask with a stopper. Which statement is correct?

(A) Experiment 1 breaks Lavoisier's law because the mass went down.
(B) In experiment 1, the 4.4 g difference is the mass of CO₂ that went into the atmosphere.
(C) In experiment 2, the mass will be 105.6 g because gases have no mass.
(D) In both, the mass goes down because fizzing destroys matter.
(E) In experiment 2, the internal pressure will go down.

**Answer: B.** Before it was 100 + 10 = 110.0 g and after it was 105.6 g. The difference, 4.4 g, is the CO₂ that escaped (44 g/mol, so 4.4 g = 0.1 mol). In the closed flask the mass would stay at 110.0 g.

### Question 6

A mineral water label says 30 mg/L of calcium ions. A person drank 500 mL in one period and 250 mL in another. What mass of calcium did they take in?

(A) 7.5 mg  (B) 15.0 mg  (C) 22.5 mg  (D) 30.0 mg  (E) 45.0 mg

**Answer: C.** That is 500 + 250 = 750 mL, or 3/4 of a liter. If 1 L has 30 mg, 3/4 of a liter has 3/4 × 30 = 22.5 mg.

### Question 7

He, Ne, Ar and Kr are in group 18 (noble gases). What explains their low reactivity under normal conditions?

(A) They easily form ionic bonds by receiving several electrons.
(B) Their outer shell is already stable, with 8 electrons (or 2 for helium).
(C) Their tiny atomic radius stops protons from moving.
(D) They exist as diatomic molecules held by hydrogen bonds.
(E) They have no neutrons in the nucleus.

**Answer: B.** Their outer shell is already full, so they don't need to bond with other elements to be stable, and they exist as gases made of single atoms.
